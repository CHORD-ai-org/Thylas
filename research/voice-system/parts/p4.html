Section 5Key paper analysis

Twenty-two works are analysed against a fixed schema. Evidence class follows Section 2.3; "basis" records what this review actually consulted, and no entry claims a full reading that did not occur.

Key paper 1 · Area I

The role of tutoring in problem solving

Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976). Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 17(2), 89–100. [[wood1976]]

Problem
What does a tutor actually do that a learner cannot do alone, and how is that support organised in interaction?
Theory
Scaffolding as a contingent, interactional achievement rather than a property of a task or a tool; the learner is treated as constructing competence inside a supported region.
Method
Observational analysis of tutorial interaction with young children on a construction task; qualitative identification of tutorial functions.
Findings
Six functions characterise effective tutoring: recruiting interest, reducing degrees of freedom, maintaining direction, marking critical features, controlling frustration, and demonstrating. Each is contingent — its value depends on the learner's state at that moment, and the same act can be helpful or intrusive.
Limits
Small-sample, single-task observation with a human tutor; no outcome measures, no comparison condition, no account of how the tutor's diagnosis is made.
Relevance
Supplies the functional vocabulary for what an AI's contributions should be for — not "answering" but recruiting, narrowing, marking, and demonstrating. Its central claim is also the strongest argument against fixed intervention policies: contingency cannot be pre-scheduled.
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract; the six functions are the paper's canonical contribution.

Key paper 2 · Area I

Exploring the assistance dilemma in experiments with cognitive tutors

Koedinger & Aleven (2007). Educational Psychology Review, 19(3), 239–264. [[koed2007]]

Problem
When should an instructional system provide assistance, and how much, given that assistance can both enable and displace learning?
Theory
Cognitive load theory and the "assistance dilemma": help trades immediate success against the cognitive processing that produces learning.
Method
Programmatic review of experiments run within the Cognitive Tutor / Andes lineage, plus a methodological argument about how to design studies that separate performance from learning.
Findings
Assistance choices reliably change the performance–learning balance; there is no global optimum. The paper argues that the field's default justification — "help helps" — conflates what learners accomplish with what they learn, and proposes experimental designs that hold task success constant while varying the cognitive work required.
Limits
Confined to well-structured quantitative domains with step-level models; the "learning" measures are mostly near-transfer post-tests; no treatment of open-ended or creative work.
Relevance
This is the paper that makes Lemma's H1 coherent: the argument for selective participation is not that help is bad but that help has a hidden cost that only delayed measurement reveals. It also explains why a product optimising session success will drift toward over-assistance.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract and the framework's subsequent use in the tutoring literature.

Key paper 3 · Area I

The expertise reversal effect

Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003). Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 23–31. [[kalyuga2003]]

Problem
Why do instructional techniques that work for novices fail, or reverse, for more knowledgeable learners?
Theory
Cognitive load theory: once schemas exist, instructional support that compensates for their absence becomes redundant and consumes working memory that could be used for learning.
Method
Review and synthesis of experiments across domains (including technical and physics instruction) comparing supported and unsupported formats at different levels of prior knowledge.
Findings
Support that is highly effective for low-knowledge learners loses effectiveness and can produce worse outcomes for high-knowledge learners. The reversal is not a small interaction; in several studies the sign of the effect changes.
Limits
Expertise is usually operationalised by a domain test administered at one point; the effect is documented mostly for structured instructional materials, not for dialogue or assistance in open tasks.
Relevance
Directly constrains any "adaptive" participation policy: adaptation must be to what the learner has automated, not to task difficulty or to error rate. It also predicts that a system tuned on novice behaviour will become intrusive as the same learner gains competence — the fading problem, in a sharper form.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 4 · Area I

Productive failure

Kapur (2008). Cognition and Instruction, 26(3), 379–424. [[kapur2008]]

Problem
Can attempting problems before receiving instruction — and failing — produce better learning than being taught the procedure first?
Theory
Generation and failure as preparation for learning: prior attempts build the representational resources that later instruction can organise.
Method
Randomised classroom study with eleventh-grade science students in a computer-supported collaborative setting; two sequences compared (ill-structured then well-structured vs. well-structured throughout), with individual post-tests.
Findings
The generation-first groups produced poor solutions and struggled to define and analyse the problems, yet outperformed the comparison group on subsequent well-structured problem solving. The failure was real and the benefit was delayed, appearing after instruction rather than during the generative phase.
Limits
One topic (Newtonian kinematics), one age group, group-based generation; the mechanism is inferred rather than measured, and later work has shown the effect is sensitive to design details.
Relevance
Gives the strongest theoretical case for STAY QUIET during the learner's generative attempts. It also reframes what an AI should do after failure: not rescue the attempt, but use the attempt as the material for instruction. A tutor that prevents the failure removes the preparation.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 5 · Area I

Learning versus performance: An integrative review

Soderstrom & Bjork (2015). Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10(2), 176–199. [[soder2015]]

Problem
Why is performance during instruction an unreliable index of whether learning has occurred?
Theory
The learning–performance distinction, grounded in desirable difficulties: conditions that slow acquisition often improve retention and transfer, and vice versa.
Method
Integrative review across experimental psychology, motor learning and education.
Findings
Numerous manipulations improve performance while impairing retention, and impair performance while improving retention. The review argues that instruction should be evaluated on durable changes in comprehension and skill, not on in-session success, and that learners' own judgements of learning are similarly biased.
Limits
Most primary evidence comes from laboratory tasks with short retention intervals; the translation to months-long learning in open domains is argued rather than demonstrated.
Relevance
This is the measurement rule for the whole Voice System. Any claim that a participation policy "works" must specify which of the four outcomes it improves — in-session performance, learning during the task, independent performance afterwards, or transfer — because these can move in opposite directions.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 6 · Area I

Help seeking and help design in interactive learning environments

Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer & Wallace (2003). Review of Educational Research, 73(3), 277–320. [[aleven2003]]

Problem
Do learners use on-demand help effectively, and what determines whether they do?
Theory
Help-seeking as a metacognitive and motivational strategy, situated between self-regulated learning and the design of the help system.
Method
Selective review of studies in interactive learning environments, organised by system factors, learner factors, and their interaction.
Findings
Effective help-seeking is associated with better learning, but learners frequently do not seek help when they need it, and seek it in ways that do not support learning. The review identifies help avoidance (driven by self-esteem and social comparison), executive help-seeking (asking for the answer), and instrumental help-seeking (asking for the principle) as distinct patterns, and argues that design and metacognitive skill interact.
Limits
Review rather than meta-analysis; the primary studies largely concern structured domains and menu-based hint systems, not open dialogue.
Relevance
It is the empirical basis for Lemma's central tension. H4 assumes learners can steer participation; this paper shows the steering behaviour is systematically biased. A purely learner-initiated design will under-serve the learners who most need support, and a proactive design must avoid training executive help-seeking.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 7 · Area I

The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems

VanLehn (2011). Educational Psychologist, 46(4), 197–221. [[vanlehn2011]]

Problem
How much does tutoring help, and does finer-grained interaction produce larger gains?
Theory
The "interaction hypothesis" — that effectiveness depends on the granularity of the interaction loop — tested against the alternative that the tutor's knowledge or pedagogy is what matters.
Method
Review of controlled experiments comparing human tutoring, computer tutoring and no tutoring, with computer systems classified by interaction granularity (answer-based, step-based, substep-based).
Findings
Tutoring of any kind beats no tutoring, but the curve flattens: answer-based systems are the weakest, and step-based and substep-based systems are roughly comparable to each other and to human tutors. The strong version of the interaction hypothesis — that more granular dialogue yields proportionally more learning — is not supported.
Limits
Confined to well-structured domains with measurable steps; comparisons are across studies rather than within one design; "human tutoring" varies enormously in quality.
Relevance
Sets a realistic expectation for Lemma: the marginal value of a richer conversational layer is not guaranteed to exceed the value of a well-structured step-level interaction. It argues for measuring the incremental benefit of voice and initiative over a competent non-conversational baseline.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 8 · Area I

Active-constructive-interactive: A conceptual framework for differentiating learning activities

Chi (2009). Topics in Cognitive Science, 1(1), 73–105. [[chi2009]]

Problem
Are "active", "constructive" and "interactive" distinct, and do they predict different learning outcomes?
Theory
A framework that defines the three in terms of observable overt activity and the underlying processes it licenses, generating a testable ordering hypothesis.
Method
Conceptual analysis with a review of existing evidence; the paper's contribution is definitional and predictive rather than a new experiment.
Findings
The three are distinguishable, and the framework predicts interactive > constructive > active for learning. Interaction is not merely more engaging; it requires the learner to produce inferences and have them taken up by a partner.
Limits
The ordering is a hypothesis, not an established law; "interactive" is defined in a way that requires a genuinely responsive partner, which is exactly what is uncertain about an AI.
Relevance
Gives Lemma a principled reason to want interaction rather than polished explanation: the value is in what the learner must generate. It also implies a design test for each AI turn — does it elicit a constructive or interactive response, or does it merely elicit acknowledgement?
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 9 · Area I

AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT in an authentic educational setting

Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti (2025). Scientific Reports, 15, Article 17458. [[kestin2025]]

Problem
How does a purpose-built generative AI tutor compare with a well-taught active-learning class on learning and on learner experience?
Theory
Pedagogical design, not model capability, determines tutoring quality; the AI tutor was built to implement the same research-based practices used in the comparison class.
Method
Randomised controlled trial with college students in an authentic course; comparison against in-class active learning with the same content and pedagogical approach; measures of learning and of engagement and motivation.
Findings
Students learned significantly more in significantly less time with the AI tutor than in the active-learning class, and reported higher engagement and motivation. Because the tutor was constrained to the same pedagogy as the class, the result is evidence about delivery medium and individualisation, not about unconstrained model access.
Limits
One course and institution; the tutor was custom-built with expert pedagogical design; outcomes are course-level assessments, and the abstract does not report delayed retention or transfer.
Relevance
The strongest pro-AI-tutoring evidence available, and simultaneously the strongest argument for H1's mechanism: what produced the gain was a tutor that refused to behave like a chatbot. It does not show that proactive intervention beats learner-initiated help — the tutor remained largely learner-driven — so it supports the "constrained assistance" half of Lemma's thesis, not the "AI decides when to speak" half.
Class
Direct   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 10 · Area I

Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics

Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman (2025). PNAS, 122(26). [[bastani2025]]

Problem
Does access to a generative AI tutor improve learning, and does tutor design change the answer?
Theory
Tool design as the operative variable: an unconstrained assistant supports task completion, while a constrained one is intended to protect the learner's own processing.
Method
Field experiment with nearly a thousand high school mathematics students; two conditions differing in interface and prompting — a standard chat interface and a version with learning safeguards — with performance measured during access and after access was withdrawn.
Findings
Access improved practice performance substantially (+48% for the base condition, +127% with safeguards). Once access was removed, students who had used the base condition performed worse than students who had never had access (−17%), while the safeguarded condition largely avoided this. The authors interpret the pattern as use of the model as a crutch during practice.
Limits
Single domain and short horizon; the mechanism is inferred from performance patterns rather than observed; the "tutor" condition is a prompting configuration, not a validated pedagogy; a published correction accompanies the paper.
Relevance
The clearest empirical demonstration of the assistance dilemma in the LLM era, and the strongest single justification for Lemma's control over participation. It also shows the effect is design-dependent: the same model produced opposite learning outcomes depending on whether it was allowed to answer.
Class
Direct   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 11 · Area II

New frontiers: Regulating learning in CSCL

Järvelä & Hadwin (2013). Educational Psychologist, 48(1), 25–39. [[jarvela2013]]

Problem
How do individuals and groups regulate collaborative learning, and what should CSCL environments support?
Theory
The tripartite model: self-regulated, co-regulated and socially shared regulation, with regulation treated as distributed across participants and artefacts.
Method
Conceptual synthesis of regulation research and CSCL design, with illustrative process data.
Findings
The three forms are analytically distinct and require different support. Co-regulation is temporary and asymmetric — one participant scaffolds another's regulation — whereas shared regulation is jointly owned and requires negotiated goals. Most collaborative groups do not spontaneously achieve shared regulation.
Limits
Conceptual; the constructs are hard to operationalise in real time, and the paper does not specify how a non-human participant could occupy the co-regulating role.
Relevance
Gives Lemma the correct target for intervention: the learner's planning, monitoring and evaluation, not only the content. It also warns that "shared" regulation with an AI is a category error unless the AI has goals it can negotiate — a limit Lemma should design around rather than claim past.
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 12 · Area II

High-level co-regulation in collaborative learning: How does it emerge and how is it sustained?

Volet, Summers & Thurman (2009). Learning and Instruction, 19(2), 128–143. [[volet2009]]

Problem
Under what conditions does co-regulation that addresses planning, monitoring and evaluation — rather than content — emerge and persist?
Theory
Co-regulation as a situated, interactional accomplishment, analysed at the level of conversational episodes.
Method
Process analysis of small-group collaborative learning, coding regulatory talk over time and relating it to group characteristics and task conditions.
Findings
High-level co-regulation appeared in some groups and not others, clustered in particular episodes rather than being continuously present, and its maintenance depended on the quality of the other-regulation available. Content-level help was far more common than regulation-level help.
Limits
Small number of groups in one educational setting; coding of regulatory talk is interpretive; no experimental manipulation.
Relevance
Two design consequences. First, regulation-level contributions are rare in human collaboration, which means an AI that supplies them is doing something humans mostly do not — neither obviously good nor bad, but untested. Second, co-regulation decays, so a single well-timed regulatory prompt is unlikely to persist; persistence is a design requirement, not a nicety.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 13 · Area II

The concept of hybrid human-AI regulation

Molenaar (2022). Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, 3, Article 100070. [[molenaar2022]]

Problem
How should an AI support a learner's self-regulated learning without taking the regulation away from the learner?
Theory
Hybrid human-AI regulation, built on a detect–diagnose–act architecture and on the SRL/co-regulation tradition.
Method
Conceptual framework with an illustrative design for young learners; no outcome data.
Findings
Argues that AI support should target the learner's regulatory processes (planning, monitoring, evaluation) rather than task content, and that the system's first task is detection and diagnosis of the learner's regulatory state. Frames the resulting arrangement as hybrid rather than delegated: the learner retains regulatory ownership while the system contributes detection and prompting.
Limits
Purely conceptual; the detect–diagnose–act chain assumes accurate inference of internal regulatory states from observable behaviour, which is the hardest part and is not validated here.
Relevance
The closest existing theoretical statement of what Lemma is attempting, and a useful corrective to content-centric tutoring. Its weak point is also Lemma's: the diagnosis step. If the system cannot tell planning from floundering, the participation decision has no reliable input.
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 14 · Area III

Principles of mixed-initiative user interfaces

Horvitz (1999). Proceedings of CHI '99, 159–166. [[horvitz1999]]

Problem
When should an intelligent system act on its own, and when should it defer to the user?
Theory
Expected utility of initiative: value of the action, discounted by the probability that the system's inference about the user's goals is correct, minus the cost of the action including the user's cost of noticing, understanding and correcting it.
Method
Conceptual framework illustrated by the LookOut system for scheduling and meeting management; no controlled evaluation of the principles.
Findings
Twelve design principles, of which four carry most weight for Lemma: consider uncertainty about goals explicitly; weigh the costs and benefits of autonomous action; use cheap dialogue to reduce uncertainty rather than guessing; and make correction of wrong inferences inexpensive. Also argues that initiative should be continuous rather than alternating, coupling automation with direct manipulation.
Limits
Assumes a utility function the designer can write down; the illustrative domain has clear task success criteria and no learning objective; no empirical test of whether the calculus improves outcomes.
Relevance
The structural backbone of Section 6. Its limitation is Lemma's central research problem: the value term must be learning value, which is not observable in the session and is often negatively correlated with task utility.
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 15 · Area III

Ten challenges for making automation a "team player" in joint human-agent activity

Klein, Woods, Bradshaw, Hoffman & Feltovich (2004). IEEE Intelligent Systems, 19(6), 91–95. [[klein2004]]

Problem
What does it take for automation to participate in joint activity rather than merely execute functions?
Theory
Joint activity and common ground from cognitive systems engineering: participants must maintain a basic compact, coordinate through directives and alerts, and be able to direct and be directed.
Method
Conceptual analysis synthesising human-centred computing principles and the automation literature.
Findings
Ten requirements, including a basic compact to participate and to signal breakdowns, directability, observability of the agent's state and intent, predictability, and the ability to negotiate goals rather than only execute them. The paper's core claim is that these are properties of the joint system, not of the agent alone.
Limits
Written for safety-critical operational settings with trained operators and explicit shared goals; no empirical test of the challenges individually.
Relevance
Supplies the missing requirement in most mixed-initiative designs: a well-calibrated intervenor that cannot be redirected is not a partner. The observability requirement is the theoretical basis for making AI silence and AI uncertainty visible to the learner.
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 16 · Area IV

If not now, when? The effects of interruption at different moments within task execution

Adamczyk & Bailey (2004). Proceedings of CHI '04, 271–278. [[adamczyk2004]]

Problem
Does the moment of an interruption within a task change its cost, independently of the interruption's content?
Theory
Task models derived from event perception; interruption cost is predicted from the cognitive load at the moment of interruption, with boundaries between subtasks presumed cheaper than interior moments.
Method
Controlled experiment; participants performed a hierarchical task and were interrupted at moments chosen from task models; measures of task performance, emotional state, and social attribution toward the interrupter.
Findings
Identical interruptions produced different outcomes depending on timing. Interruptions at coarser boundaries were less disruptive and produced more positive social attribution than interruptions inside a subtask, which increased annoyance and reduced the perceived competence of the interrupting party.
Limits
Laboratory task with well-defined structure; short horizon; the "interrupter" was a system, so social attribution is a proxy; no learning measure.
Relevance
Establishes that Lemma's timing decision is separable from its content decision, and that timing has a social as well as a performance cost. A question asked at the wrong moment damages the learner's model of the AI, not just their solution.

Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 17 · Area IV

The effect of interruption duration and demand on resuming suspended goals

Monk, Trafton & Boehm-Davis (2008). Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 14(4), 299–313. [[monk2008]]

Problem
What determines how long it takes to resume a task after an interruption?
Theory
The memory-for-goals model: suspended goals decay and must be re-activated, so resumption cost should increase with interruption duration and with the demand of the interrupting task.
Method
Three experiments using an interleaved-tasks interruption paradigm in a hierarchical interactive task, varying interruption duration and cognitive demand.
Findings
Longer and more demanding interruptions produced longer resumption times, consistent with decay plus rehearsal. For durations up to about one minute the profile supported a decay account, and the interaction between duration and demand indicated that rehearsal mitigates the loss.
Limits
Short, well-structured laboratory tasks; resumption measured in seconds; no test of whether resumption cost translates into learning cost.
Relevance
Gives a concrete reason to keep AI contributions short: duration is itself a cost parameter. It also implies that an AI turn requiring a reply is more expensive than one that does not, because the reply extends the interruption.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 18 · Area IV

Meta-analysis of the modality effect

Ginns (2005). Learning and Instruction, 15(4), 313–331. [[ginns2005]]

Problem
Is the modality effect — spoken rather than written text accompanying visual material — reliable, and what moderates it?
Theory
Cognitive load theory's modality principle: distributing processing across visual and auditory channels reduces load when the material is otherwise visually saturated.
Method
Meta-analysis of controlled experiments comparing audio-plus-visual with visual-only presentations.
Findings
A reliable overall advantage for the modality format, with substantial heterogeneity. The advantage is conditional on pacing and on the length of the spoken segment: longer, system-paced audio erodes it.
Limits
Primary studies use short instructional segments in laboratory or classroom settings; outcomes are immediate comprehension and transfer tests, not long-term retention; little coverage of interactive or contingent speech.
Relevance
The strongest quantitative support for voice as an output channel in Lemma — but only under conditions Lemma must engineer: short segments, learner control of pacing, and visual material that is genuinely visual.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract and the meta-analytic literature citing it.

Key paper 19 · Area IV

Cognitive load theory, modality of presentation and the transient information effect

Leahy & Sweller (2011). Applied Cognitive Psychology, 25(6), 943–951. [[leahy2011]]

Problem
When does the modality effect reverse, and why?
Theory
Transience: spoken information disappears and cannot be re-inspected, so long audio imposes working-memory costs that written text does not.
Method
Two experiments with primary-school students learning to read a temperature graph from either audio-plus-diagram or written-text-plus-diagram materials.
Findings
The visual-text condition outperformed the audio condition, reversing the usual modality effect. The authors attribute the reversal to the auditory text being too long and transient to be processed comfortably, so that its working-memory cost exceeded the benefit of freeing the visual channel.
Limits
Children, one task, short instructional materials; the manipulation confounds modality with transience, which is the point but also limits generalisation.
Relevance
The single most important constraint on Lemma's voice output: speech is a poor medium for content the learner may need to revisit, and the boundary is not "short vs long" in words but "can the learner re-inspect it". It predicts that spoken explanations of more than a sentence or two will underperform equivalent on-screen text.

Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 20 · Area V

"Like having a really bad PA": The gulf between user expectation and experience of conversational agents

Luger & Sellen (2016). Proceedings of CHI '16, 5286–5297. [[luger2016]]

Problem
How do people actually use conversational agents, and how does that differ from what conversation promises?
Theory
Conversation as an interaction metaphor, examined against observed use.
Method
Interviews with fourteen users of commercial conversational agents, analysed qualitatively.
Findings
Expectations were shaped by science-fiction depictions of conversation and were systematically disappointed. Use was dominated by simple commands and information lookup rather than dialogue; users described the agents as brittle, limited in context, and socially inept. The paper argues the conversational metaphor sets expectations the technology cannot meet.
Limits
Fourteen participants, 2016 technology, self-report; no learning context and no task performance data.
Relevance
A direct warning against justifying voice by naturalness. It also predicts a specific failure for Lemma: if the voice layer promises conversation and delivers commands or monologues, the learner's trust drops faster than if the layer had promised less.
Class
Indirect   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 21 · Area VI

Grounding in communication

Clark & Brennan (1991). In Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition (pp. 127–149). APA. [[clarkbrennan1991]]

Problem
What does it take for two participants to establish that they have understood each other, and how do media change that work?
Theory
Grounding: communication succeeds when participants reach mutual belief that understanding has occurred, at a cost paid jointly; media differ in the costs they impose.
Method
Conceptual framework with analysis of media properties (copresence, visibility, audibility, cotemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, reviewability, revisability).
Findings
Grounding is collaborative and cost-sensitive; the least collaborative effort principle selects the cheapest sufficient evidence of understanding. Media that permit review and revision lower grounding cost for complex content, while media with cotemporality and visibility lower it for reference. The framework predicts which costs dominate in which medium.
Limits
Analytic rather than experimental; assumes cooperative participants with aligned goals; says nothing about a participant whose understanding cannot be inspected.
Relevance
The core theoretical justification for H2. It converts "workspace grounding" from a metaphor into a cost claim: shared visual access removes the need to describe referents, and reviewability removes the cost of transient speech. It also predicts the exact failure of voice-only interaction in a workspace.
Class
Theoretical   Basis: abstract.

Key paper 22 · Area VII

Distance matters

Olson & Olson (2000). Human-Computer Interaction, 15(2–3), 139–178. [[olson2000]]

Problem
Why does geographically distributed collaboration remain difficult despite abundant bandwidth?
Theory
Common ground as the limiting resource; distance in space, time, scale and culture raises the cost of maintaining it.
Method
Synthesis of a decade of field and laboratory studies of collocated and remote collaboration.
Findings
Technology readiness is necessary but insufficient; the decisive factors are common ground, the coupling of the work, collaboration readiness, and culture. Remote work succeeds when tasks are loosely coupled and common ground can be maintained cheaply; tightly coupled work with high common-ground requirements remains difficult.
Limits
Written before persistent AI partners existed; the unit is teams of humans with shared organisational goals; no learning outcomes.
Relevance
Reframes continuity as a common-ground maintenance problem. A cross-session AI partner is not valuable because it remembers, but because it lowers the cost of re-establishing shared understanding — and unverified accumulated context can raise that cost by creating false confidence. It also suggests coupling as a design variable: the more tightly the AI's contributions are coupled to the learner's current move, the more common ground is required.

Indirect   Basis: abstract.
