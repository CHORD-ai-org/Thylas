Each subsection states what the area establishes, what it does not, and what it implies for a participation policy. Detailed paper-level extraction is in Section 5; the tables here cover the supporting corpus.

4.1 Learning, scaffolding and learner agency

The starting point is that assistance is a relation, not a quantity. Wood, Bruner and Ross described tutoring as a set of six contingent functions — recruitment, reduction of degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control, demonstration — and stressed that the tutor's task is to keep the learner in the region where success is possible with help and impossible without it [[wood1976]]. Contingency is doing the theoretical work: the same act is scaffolding or interference depending on the learner's state at that moment.

Van de Pol, Volman and Beishuizen's decade review confirms the contingency claim empirically and adds the diagnostic problem: effective scaffolding depends on the teacher's continuous diagnosis of understanding, and this diagnosis is the least reliable part of practice [[vandepol2010]]. That is a warning about the difficulty of the judgement, not about its value.

The assistance dilemma gives the trade-off a measurable form. Koedinger and Aleven's experiments with cognitive tutors varied how much help was given, when, and in what form; the consistent result is that assistance choices shift the balance between performance during practice and learning that survives it, so "more help" is not a coherent design goal [[koed2007]]. The sharpest version of this is expertise reversal: support that compensates for missing schemas becomes redundant load once the schemas exist, and redundancy costs learning [[kalyuga2003]]. Soderstrom and Bjork's review explains why this is easy to miss in product metrics: performance during training is a biased index of learning, and conditions that slow the learner down often produce more durable outcomes [[soder2015]].

Two lines of work push against the assumption that the AI should resolve difficulty. Kapur's productive failure studies show that attempting ill-structured problems before instruction, and failing, can prepare students to learn more from subsequent instruction than being taught the well-structured procedure first [[kapur2008]]. Aleven and colleagues' review of help-seeking shows the complementary behavioural fact: learners in interactive environments do not use help well — they avoid it when it would threaten self-esteem, request it when the answer is within reach, and rarely use it strategically [[aleven2003]]. Roll and colleagues showed that this is trainable: metacognitive feedback aimed at help-seeking behaviour improved both the behaviour and subsequent learning [[roll2011]].

Where does that leave "the learner retains control"? It leaves it as a value, not a mechanism. Learner control is well motivated — autonomy support, avoidance of dependency, respect for the learner's own goals — but it is not self-executing, because the population least likely to request help appropriately is precisely the population that most needs it [[aleven2003]]. Any design in which the learner must initiate inherits that bias.

What this area establishes

  • Assistance effects are contingent on the learner's current knowledge and on the moment of intervention, and can reverse sign [[kalyuga2003,koed2007]].
  • Performance and learning must be measured separately, and they frequently diverge [[soder2015]].
  • Learners' spontaneous help-seeking is systematically suboptimal, and trainable [[aleven2003]].

What it does not establish

  • That an AI should decide when to help. Almost all of this work studies human tutors or tutors with hand-authored, narrow-domain models; the judgement of "current understanding" was made by a person or by a domain model, not by a general-purpose language model.
  • That withholding help is better than offering it. The productive-failure results concern the sequence of generation and instruction, not the behaviour of a partner who could intervene at any moment.

4.2 Co-regulation and human–AI collaboration

The regulatory framing is the most useful available vocabulary for what Lemma wants, because it locates the unit of analysis between two parties rather than inside either. Järvelä and Hadwin's tripartite distinction — self-, co-, and socially shared regulation — separates three things that product language tends to blur: a learner regulating their own work; a partner temporarily supporting that regulation; and a group whose regulation is jointly owned [[jarvela2013]]. Lemma's AI is designed for the middle category, but its workspace ambitions pull it toward the third, and the third requires capacities (shared goal commitment, socio-emotional regulation, negotiated task ownership) that an AI does not have.

Co-regulation has an identifiable microstructure. Volet's analyses of collaborative learning found that high-level co-regulation involves one partner addressing the other's planning, monitoring or evaluation — not merely answering the content question — and that it emerged in some groups and then decayed [[volet2009]]. Roschelle and Teasley's joint problem space explains why decay is the default: the shared representation has to be continuously maintained, and it degrades when participants stop checking it [[roschelle1995]].

Grounding theory supplies the cost model for that maintenance. Clark and Brennan's formulation — that grounding requires the participants to reach mutual belief at a cost paid jointly, and that the medium determines which costs dominate [[clarkbrennan1991]] — is arguably the single most useful framework for the workspace claim, because it predicts exactly which costs a shared visual context removes. When both parties can see the object, reference can be short and deixis replaces description; when they cannot, the same reference requires a description, a check, and often a repair [[clarkwg1986]].

Molenaar's hybrid human-AI regulation is the clearest existing attempt to move this into AI design, using a detect–diagnose–act structure in which the target of support is the learner's regulatory process rather than the task content [[molenaar2022]]. Its importance for Lemma is that it reframes the participation question: the AI is not choosing whether to help with the mathematics, it is choosing whether to support planning, monitoring or evaluation, and those have different interruption costs.

Sebanz, Bekkering and Knoblich's work on joint action adds a constraint from motor cognition: co-representation of a partner's task is automatic and can interfere with one's own performance [[sebanz2006]]. In a learning setting this predicts a cost that is usually invisible — knowing that a partner is watching and may act changes how the learner represents the task, even when the partner does nothing. This is the theoretical basis for treating "AI stays quiet but present" as a distinct condition from "no AI", not as a control condition.

4.3 Mixed-initiative interaction and selective participation

Mixed initiative is the area with the most directly usable structure, and also the one with the largest gap between theory and learning evidence. Horvitz's 1999 paper remains the canonical statement: the system should act when the expected value of acting exceeds the expected cost, where value is discounted by the probability that the system's inference about the user's needs is correct, and cost includes the user's effort to notice, interpret and correct the action [[horvitz1999]]. Three of his principles are effectively design requirements for Lemma: consider uncertainty about the user's goals explicitly; use cheap interaction to reduce that uncertainty rather than guessing; and make correction cheap because some guesses will be wrong.

The lineage matters. Carbonell's SCHOLAR system already embedded the idea that a tutoring program should take initiative when the student's state warranted it and yield when it did not [[carbonell1970]]; Allen, Guinn and Horvitz generalised the concept across dialogue systems [[allen1999]]; Terveen framed human–computer collaboration as a matter of shared goals and adjustable initiative rather than command [[terveen1995]]. The idea is therefore not new to AI, and its repeated re-discovery in conversational products has not been matched by evidence about learning.

Automation research supplies the control vocabulary that mixed initiative lacks. Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens separate what is automated from how much, and make the crucial point that automation does not replace human activity so much as restructure it, creating new coordination demands [[para2000]]. Parasuraman and Riley's taxonomy of use, misuse, disuse and abuse names the ways operators adapt badly: over-trust, complacency, abandonment [[parariley1997]]. Endsley and Kiris show the structural consequence of high automation: with the operator out of the loop, situation awareness degrades and takeover is slow and error-prone [[endsley1995]].

For a learning agent, the "out-of-the-loop" finding has an exact analogue that the literature has not tested: a learner who has ceded the monitoring function to the AI may be unable to resume it when the AI is unavailable, wrong, or simply silent. This is not the same claim as "dependency"; it is a claim about situation awareness of one's own problem state, and it is testable with existing instruments.

Klein and colleagues' "team player" framing is the best available corrective to purely probabilistic accounts. Their ten challenges include a basic compact to participate in joint activity, directability (the human can redirect the agent), observability (the agent's state and intent are visible), and predictability [[klein2004]]. The lesson for Lemma is that a well-calibrated intervenor that cannot be redirected is not a team player. Amershi's guidelines translate these into product-level obligations, notably: make clear what the system can do; make clear why it did what it did; support efficient invocation and dismissal; and adapt cautiously over time [[amershi2019]].

Shneiderman and Maes' debate is worth keeping in view because it anticipates the current argument. Maes argued for agents that act on the user's behalf; Shneiderman argued that direct manipulation preserves control and predictability and that autonomous agents shift the burden of monitoring onto the user [[shneimaes1997]]. Lemma's workspace is the direct-manipulation side of that debate and its voice layer is the agent side; the paper's value is that it predicts the specific friction at the seam.

The gap that matters most. Mixed-initiative theory tells you how to decide, but the value term it assumes is the user's task utility. Learning value is not task utility: the contribution that maximises the quality of the current solution may minimise the learning. Every application of Horvitz's framework to education therefore requires replacing the value term, and no paper in this corpus does so empirically.
