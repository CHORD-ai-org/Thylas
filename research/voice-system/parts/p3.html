4.4 Attention, cognitive load and interruption

Interruption research is where the cost side of Lemma's ledger is quantified. Its central finding is that the cost of an interruption is not a property of its content but of its relation to the task's structure. Adamczyk and Bailey varied only the moment of interruption within an identical task and found differences in performance, annoyance, and the social attribution the user made about the interrupter; boundaries between subtasks were consistently safer than moments inside one [[adamczyk2004]]. Monk, Trafton and Boehm-Davis showed that resumption time rises with both the duration and the cognitive demand of the interruption, and that this profile is consistent with a memory-for-goals account in which the suspended goal decays and must be reinstated [[monk2008,altmann2002]]. Iqbal and Horvitz's field data show the same structure outside the laboratory: interruptions in real work produce measurable resumption lags and frequently derail the suspended task altogether [[iqbal2007]].

Two additional findings make the accounting worse than a simple delay. Leroy's attention-residue experiments show that switching costs persist after the person has returned, because part of attention remains with the interrupting task [[leroy2009]]. And Bailey and Konstan's measurements show that even brief interruptions raise error rates and negative affect, not just completion time [[bailey2006]]. A conversational intervention, which requires the learner to parse a sentence, decide whether it is relevant, and formulate a response, is not brief in this sense.

The countervailing evidence is that interruption cost is not uniform across channels. Multiple-resource theory holds that tasks drawing on different perceptual and cognitive resources interfere less than tasks drawing on the same ones [[wickens2002]]. Speech input and output occupy auditory and verbal resources; visual workspace manipulation occupies visual and spatial resources. On this account, a short spoken intervention during visual manipulation should cost less than a text panel that must be read in the same visual field. This is the strongest theoretical argument for voice in Lemma — and it is a theory about resource competition, not about naturalness.

The same theory predicts where voice fails. If the learner is reading prose, the verbal channel is already occupied, and spoken assistance competes directly with the material. The modality effect — better learning from spoken rather than written text accompanying diagrams [[ginns2005]] — holds for short, self-paced segments and reverses when the audio is long and transient, because the learner cannot re-inspect what has already been said [[leahy2011]]. Singh, Marcus and Ayres showed that segmenting transient audio mitigates but does not eliminate the disadvantage [[singh2012]]. The practical implication is unusually clear: speech is a bad container for content the learner may need to revisit.

Two further lines bear on the "continuous help" worry. Risko and Gilbert's review of cognitive offloading shows that people offload strategically to reduce effort, and that offloading is often beneficial for the immediate task while reducing the internal representations needed later [[risko2016]]. Lee and colleagues' survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking and a shift from producing to verifying, with participants describing the effort as moving rather than disappearing [[lee2025]]. Neither study is about learning, and neither measures retention; both are consistent with the concern that continuous availability changes what the user does with their own mind.

4.5 Voice as an interaction modality

The literature on voice is less about speech than about modality assignment: which content belongs in which channel. Oviatt's work is the most useful corrective to assumptions that speech is natural and therefore preferable. Her "ten myths" paper argues, among other things, that users do not uniformly prefer speech, that multimodal integration is technically and cognitively complex, and that the modality that looks easiest for the designer is often the one that imposes most work on the user [[oviatt1999]].

The empirical basis for assignment comes from studies of spontaneous multimodal interaction. Users given both speech and pen do not use them interchangeably: they reserve speech for descriptive and semantic content and pen for spatial and directly manipulable content, and they switch when the content type changes [[cohen1992,oviatt1997]]. This is a finding about fit, and it maps unusually well onto Lemma's architecture. Naming an assumption, stating an intention, accepting or rejecting a suggestion: descriptive, speech-suited. Selecting a term, adjusting a parameter, pointing at a step: spatial, manipulation-suited.

The counter-evidence is about expectation. Luger and Sellen's interviews with conversational-agent users documented a persistent gap between the promise of conversation and the experience of command-and-control: participants described the agents as brittle, limited, and socially awkward — "like having a really bad PA" [[luger2016]]. Porcheron and colleagues' month-long in-home study showed that voice use is embedded in social accountability: people design their utterances for overhearers, repair the device's understanding in front of others, and treat speech as a public act rather than a private command [[porcheron2018]]. Clark and colleagues' analysis of conversational quality reaches a related conclusion: conversational interaction is not a universal ideal, and users often prefer efficient, non-conversational interaction for instrumental tasks [[clark2019]].

Nass and Moon's finding that people apply social rules to computers — politeness, reciprocity, gender stereotyping — matters here in a way that is easy to overlook [[nass2000]]. If a spoken agent is treated as a social actor, then rejecting its suggestion is not a neutral interface action; it is a mildly social one. That cuts both ways for Lemma: social presence may increase engagement, and it may also raise the cost of saying "no", which is precisely the control action H4 depends on.

What does not exist. No study in this corpus examines voice as a coordination channel over a shared visual learning workspace. The nearest analogues are multimodal desktop systems from the 1990s and medical or field settings; the nearest learning evidence is about spoken versus written instructional text, which is output, not coordination. H6 is therefore a design hypothesis with plausible mechanisms and no direct test — and the report classifies it that way.

4.6 Workspace grounding and shared context

Referring is a collaborative process, not a labelling act. Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs showed that speakers and addressees iterate on a description until they reach a version both accept, and that they do so under a principle of least collaborative effort: the pair minimises total effort, not the speaker's effort [[clarkwg1986]]. In their tangram task, references became shorter and more conventional across trials as the pair accumulated shared history. Two consequences follow for Lemma. First, a learner saying "this assumption" is behaving optimally, not vaguely. Second, the system's ability to resolve such references is not a convenience feature; it is the precondition for the interaction being cheap enough to be worth having.

Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks' analysis of repair adds a normative constraint that design tends to violate. In conversation there is a strong preference for self-repair: participants give the speaker of a trouble-source the first opportunity to fix it, and other-repair is dispreferred [[schegloff1977]]. Translated to a learning workspace, an AI that immediately corrects a learner's misstatement is doing other-repair at the earliest possible point — the interactional equivalent of interrupting. The preference structure suggests a cheaper and less face-threatening move: mark the trouble and let the learner repair it.

The workspace itself is not passive. Zhang and Norman showed that external representations change the difficulty and even the form of a cognitive task, so that what a person can do depends on how the problem is laid out [[zhang1994]]. Kirsh and Maglio showed that some actions in a workspace are performed to change the cognitive situation rather than the physical one — epistemic rather than pragmatic action [[kirsh1994]]. Together these imply that a shared workspace is a cognitive resource jointly operated, not a display surface for a dialogue. An AI that treats the workspace as context for its utterances misses the point; the workspace is where a large part of the thinking happens.

Whittaker's review of "talking about things" in mediated communication provides the most directly relevant empirical summary: speech alone is often sufficient for effective conversation; visual information about the objects of work is generally more valuable than visual information about the participants; and disjoint visual perspectives undermine communication [[whittaker2003]]. For a system that has both speech and a shared view of the objects, this is close to a design brief.

Suchman's critique of plan-based models of action is the theoretical reason not to over-intellectualise the learner's intent [[suchman1987]]. Plans are resources for situated action, not determinants of it; what a person is doing is observable in the specifics of the situation rather than derivable from a plan the system has inferred. An AI that grounds its interpretation in workspace state and recent action is following Suchman's advice; one that grounds it in a model of the learner's "learning objective" is not.

4.7 Long-term collaboration and continuity

This is the thinnest area for direct evidence and the one where Lemma's ambitions most exceed the literature. Olson and Olson's decade of field and laboratory work concluded that the decisive variable in geographically distributed collaboration is not bandwidth but common ground — and that distance matters because it raises the cost of maintaining it [[olson2000]]. Their framework lists the factors that make collaboration "ready": common ground, coupling of work, collaboration technology readiness, and culture. Applied to a long-horizon AI partner, the framework predicts that continuity is valuable exactly to the extent that it reduces the cost of re-establishing common ground — and that accumulated, unverified context can be worse than none, because it produces false confidence in shared understanding.

Transactive memory supplies the mechanism. Lewis's validated scale decomposes a transactive memory system into specialisation (who knows what), credibility (how much to trust each member's expertise) and coordination (how well access is orchestrated) [[lewis2003]]. Human teams with well-developed transactive systems perform better. A learner working with an AI may develop a transactive relation in which the AI is assumed to hold certain knowledge — and the credibility component is the risk, because it must be calibrated by experience, and language models are confidently wrong at a rate that makes experience a noisy teacher.

Collaborative memory research adds a caution against assuming that shared records improve memory. Rajaram and Pereira-Pasarin's review documents both benefits (error correction, transactive gain) and costs (retrieval disruption, collaborative inhibition — groups recall less than the sum of their members' individual recall) [[rajaram2010]]. The design implication is that a persistent record of prior reasoning is not automatically an aid: it can anchor the learner to a faulty earlier framing, and it can substitute for the reconstructive effort that produces durable memory.

Schmidt and Bannon's concept of articulation work names the invisible labour of coordinating a distributed activity — scheduling, aligning representations, repairing breakdowns [[schmidt1992]]. A collaboration partner is valuable to the extent that it absorbs articulation work rather than generating it. This gives a clean test for any continuity feature: does it reduce the work the learner must do to re-enter their own prior thinking, or does it add a surface the learner must now maintain?

Honest gap. Four of the five works in this area study human teams. The one AI-specific source [[molenaar2022]] is conceptual. There is no longitudinal study in this corpus that measures learning outcomes of a persistent AI partner across sessions, and the product claim in H5 should be treated as unvalidated rather than as supported by analogy.
