Section 1Executive summary

The literature gives a strong rationale for treating AI participation as a decision, a reasonably clear account of what the decision has to trade off, and almost no direct evidence about voice in a shared learning workspace. Those three facts have different implications, and the report keeps them apart.

1. The design stance is well supported; the specific claim is not. Learning science has documented for five decades that assistance is not a monotonic good. Scaffolding works when it is contingent, graded and withdrawn [21, 43, 62], and the same help that raises performance during a task can lower retention and transfer afterwards [27, 54]. That is enough to justify Lemma's principle that participation should be a decision rather than a default. It is not evidence that an AI which deliberately withholds help learns better than one that responds whenever asked: no study in this literature compares those two policies directly. The closest evidence is indirect — AI tutors that were pedagogically constrained outperformed both unconstrained chatbots and, in one randomised trial, a well-taught active-learning class [23], while unconstrained access to the same model left students worse off than students who never had it [7].

2. The interesting decision is not three-way. The ASK / OFFER / STAY QUIET triad conflates at least five separable decisions: whether to act now; what epistemic function the act should serve; how intense it should be; which channel carries it; and how the act changes the next act. The literature on mixed initiative models the first as an expected-value calculation over value, probability of being right, and cost of being wrong [18]; the automation literature models the third as a ladder of levels rather than a switch [42]; the interruption literature shows the second-order cost is a function of when within the task the act lands, not only of what it says [1, 35]. Collapsing these into one label makes the design untestable, because any outcome can be attributed to "wrong mode" after the fact.

3. Silence is also an act, and an illegible one. Grounding theory says communication succeeds only when participants reach mutual belief that they have understood [11]. A system whose non-participation is invisible gives the learner no way to distinguish "the AI decided not to interrupt" from "the AI did not understand" — and those two states call for opposite learner responses. This is the least discussed and, we think, the most immediately actionable finding in the review: deliberate silence has to be observable to function as a collaboration signal.

4. Voice's defensible advantage is concurrency, not naturalness. The modality effect is real but conditional: spoken text paired with diagrams beats written text when the spoken segment is short and the learner can control pacing, and the advantage reverses when the audio is long and transient [15, 28, 53]. Multiple-resource theory predicts that speech is cheap precisely when the eyes and hands are busy elsewhere [61] — which is the situation in a shared workspace, and not the situation in a chat window. So the most plausible role for voice is short coordination acts layered over an object the learner is already manipulating, and the least plausible role is long spoken explanation. That is a testable prediction, and current evidence does not test it.

5. The strongest counterargument is internal to the product. H4 assumes learners can exercise control well. The help-seeking literature shows they cannot: learners under-request help when they need it, over-request when they do not, and avoid help that threatens self-esteem [2]. "Participation is a decision" is therefore not automatically learner-friendly — a system that defers entirely to learner-initiated requests inherits a known bias toward under-assistance in exactly the population Lemma targets. The design has to separate "the learner can veto" from "the learner must initiate".

A defensible reading of the evidence: the AI's contribution is valuable when it preserves the learner's generative work and reduces coordination cost. It becomes harmful when it substitutes for that work, or when its timing forces the learner to rebuild a task representation that was intact a moment earlier.

Section 2Scope and method

This is a targeted theoretical review, not a systematic review. The selection rule was relevance to a design decision, not keyword coverage.

2.1 What was searched

The review covers seven areas defined by the assignment: scaffolding and learner agency; co-regulation and collaboration; mixed-initiative interaction; attention and interruption; voice as an interaction modality; workspace grounding; and long-term continuity. Sources were peer-reviewed journals and conference proceedings in learning sciences, cognitive science, HCI, CSCW and human factors, plus a small number of book chapters that carry foundational theory.

Search proceeded in two directions. Forward from a set of anchor works whose framing is unavoidable in this space — Wood, Bruner and Ross on tutoring [62], Horvitz on mixed initiative [18], Clark and Brennan on grounding [11], Sweller and colleagues on cognitive load [24], Chi on activity types [9] — and backward from recent empirical work on generative AI in learning [7, 23, 29] to the theories those papers invoke. Every bibliographic record was resolved against Crossref; DOIs, volumes and page ranges in the bibliography are the registered values, not reconstructed ones. Where a work has no DOI (a 1987 monograph), the entry says so rather than supplying a guessed identifier.

2.2 Inclusion and exclusion

Included: work that makes a claim about when assistance helps, how collaborators coordinate, what interruption costs, or which modality carries which content. Excluded by design: speech recognition and synthesis engineering; generic chatbot usability; vendor claims about conversational interfaces; and the large literature on AI-generated content quality, which is about output, not participation.

Papers were not admitted for containing a keyword. Each had to bear on one of three questions: what the learner is doing cognitively; what a second participant can legitimately add; or what it costs to add it at a given moment. Roughly a third of the works screened were dropped for failing this test, most of them studies of "AI in education" that measured satisfaction or adoption rather than learning.

Section 3Theoretical foundations

Five explanatory clusters carry almost all of the argument. They answer different questions and should not be merged: one explains the learner, one explains what assistance can add, one explains coordination, one explains control, and one explains time.

3.1 The learner's cognitive economy

Cognitive load theory holds that instruction is constrained by a working memory that is narrow and volatile, and that learning consists in building and automating schemas in long-term memory [24]. The theory's practical corollary is uncomfortable for conversational design: anything the learner must hold in mind while solving — including the content of an utterance that has already ended — competes with the schema construction it is meant to support [24, 28] — which is why the form of a contribution matters as much as its content.

Two further concepts sharpen this. Chi's framework distinguishes active (doing), constructive (generating something not present in the material) and interactive (co-constructing with a partner) activity, and predicts that interactive > constructive > active for learning [9]. Interaction is therefore not decoration: a partner who makes the learner produce and defend inferences adds something a well-designed worksheet cannot. But Kirsh and Maglio's distinction between pragmatic and epistemic action shows that much of what looks like inefficient behaviour in a workspace — moving a tile, redrawing a diagram, rewriting an expression — is cognitive work offloaded into the world, not noise around it [25]. Zhang and Norman make the complementary point that external representations change what the task is, not merely how it is displayed [63].

The consequence for an AI is specific: interventions that tidy the learner's workspace can destroy epistemic action, and interventions that describe what the learner can already see can be pure redundancy. Both failure modes look like helpfulness from the system's side.

3.2 What assistance can add, and what it can subtract

Scaffolding began as a description of tutorial interaction, not of tools. Wood, Bruner and Ross identified what a tutor actually does: recruiting interest, reducing degrees of freedom, keeping the goal in view, marking critical features, controlling frustration, and demonstrating [62]. Every one of those is a contingent act — it presupposes a judgement about the learner's current state. Pea's later analysis warns that the metaphor has been stretched to cover any support, and that technological scaffolding differs from human scaffolding precisely in its inability to make that judgement [43].

The assistance dilemma names the resulting trade-off: help that is available and used improves performance, but the cognitive work that help replaces is often the work that produces learning [27]. Kalyuga's expertise reversal effect gives the dilemma its sharpest form — techniques that are highly effective for novices lose effectiveness and can reverse for learners with more prior knowledge, because the support becomes redundant load [21]. This is why "adaptive" cannot mean "matched to difficulty"; it has to mean "matched to what the learner has not yet automated".

Productive failure supplies the counterweight. Kapur had eleventh-grade science students attempt ill-structured problems before instruction; they failed, produced poor solutions, and then outperformed a well-structured-first comparison group on subsequent problem solving [22]. The lesson is not that failure is good, but that the generation attempt is the productive part — which means an AI that prevents failure also prevents the preparation that makes later instruction stick.

Finally, Soderstrom and Bjork's distinction between learning and performance is the methodological key to this entire area. Performance during instruction is an unreliable index of durable learning; conditions that depress performance can enhance retention, and conditions that inflate it can produce no learning at all [54]. Any evaluation of Lemma's participation policy that measures only in-session success will therefore systematically favour the more intrusive design.

3.3 Coordination: what a second participant actually does

Grounding theory treats communication as a joint project of establishing mutual belief that understanding has occurred, at a cost paid jointly [11]. Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs showed that reference is not a one-shot act but an iterative repair process governed by least collaborative effort: speakers and addressees converge on the cheapest mutually acceptable description rather than the most precise one [10]. Two implications follow for a workspace AI. First, "this assumption" is not an underspecified reference; it is a normal, efficient one, provided both parties can see the same object. Second, when the AI is unsure which object is meant, the cheapest repair is usually a brief check, not a silent guess.

Roschelle and Teasley's joint problem space adds the temporal dimension: collaborators build a shared representation of the problem — goals, current state, proposed moves — and that representation, not the individual mental models, is what the collaboration operates on [48]. Distributed cognition extends the unit of analysis to the whole system of person, artefacts and representations [17]. Under that lens, a shared workspace is not a display for a conversation; it is part of the cognitive system that is doing the learning.

Regulation theory supplies the vocabulary for who steers. Järvelä and Hadwin distinguish self-regulated, co-regulated and socially shared regulation: in co-regulation one participant temporarily scaffolds another's regulatory activity, while in shared regulation the group's regulation becomes joint property [20]. Volet's process analyses showed that high-level co-regulation — where a partner addresses the other's planning, monitoring or evaluation rather than the content alone — does emerge in some groups and not others, and is fragile once established [59]. Molenaar's hybrid human-AI regulation is the most direct attempt to apply this to AI: the system detects, diagnoses and then acts on the learner's regulation, on the argument that support should target the regulatory process rather than the task content [34].

3.4 Control: who decides, and at what level

Mixed-initiative interaction is the canonical framework for the participation problem. Horvitz's principles treat the decision to act as an expected-value calculation: the value of a contribution, multiplied by the probability that the system has correctly inferred what is needed, minus the cost of being wrong, including the cost of the learner's effort to repair it [18]. The framework also prescribes what to do under uncertainty — use cheap dialogue to resolve it, minimise the cost of wrong guesses, make correction easy, and infer timing rather than asking about it.

The automation literature generalises this from a binary to a ladder. Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens separate the type of automation (information acquisition, analysis, decision, action) from its level, running from manual through suggestion, veto and full automation [42]. The distinction matters because Lemma's ASK and OFFER are both "decision support at a low level", while an AI that edits the workspace is at a much higher level — and the failure modes differ accordingly. Parasuraman and Riley's taxonomy of use, misuse, disuse and abuse names the social failure modes: automation bias, complacency, and abandonment after a salient error [41]. Endsley and Kiris showed the structural cost of high automation levels: operators taken out of the loop lose situation awareness and are slower and worse when they must take over [14].

Klein and colleagues reframe the goal from "well-behaved automation" to "team player", with requirements that are directly translatable to a learning agent: a basic compact to participate, sufficient directability (the human can redirect it), observability (its state and intent are visible), and predictability [26]. Amershi's eighteen guidelines operationalise the same commitments for AI products, including the obligations to make clear what the system can do, to make clear why it did something, to support efficient invocation and dismissal, and to adapt over time [5].

3.5 Time: interruption, resumption, and the shape of a session

Interruption research supplies the cost side of the ledger with unusual precision. Adamczyk and Bailey interrupted users at different moments inside a task and found that timing alone changed performance, annoyance and even the perceived competence of the interrupter; boundaries between subtasks were markedly safer than moments inside one [1]. Monk, Trafton and Boehm-Davis showed that resumption cost grows with both the duration and the cognitive demand of the interruption, consistent with a memory-for-goals account in which the suspended goal decays and must be re-established [4, 35]. Leroy's attention-residue findings add that even after returning, part of attention remains on the interrupting task — the cost is not fully paid at the moment of resumption [30].

Two constraints bound the transfer of these findings to learning. First, most interruption studies use tasks with legible boundaries and short horizons; open-ended mathematical or research work has fewer natural breakpoints, so the practical question becomes how to infer a boundary rather than how to detect a known one. Second, the costs measured are task-level, whereas Lemma cares about learning-level effects that may run in the opposite direction: an interruption that harms the current solution may still improve retention if it forces retrieval.

What the theory does not settle. None of these frameworks was built for a participant that has no goals of its own, no persistent social standing, and no ability to be embarrassed by being wrong. Co-regulation theory in particular assumes two agents who each regulate themselves and negotiate goals; an AI regulates nothing of its own. Section 7 treats this asymmetry as the main threat to H3 and H4.

4.1 Learning, scaffolding and learner agency

The starting point is that assistance is a relation, not a quantity. Wood, Bruner and Ross described tutoring as a set of six contingent functions — recruitment, reduction of degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control, demonstration — and stressed that the tutor's task is to keep the learner in the region where success is possible with help and impossible without it [62]. Contingency is doing the theoretical work: the same act is scaffolding or interference depending on the learner's state at that moment.

Van de Pol, Volman and Beishuizen's decade review confirms the contingency claim empirically and adds the diagnostic problem: effective scaffolding depends on the teacher's continuous diagnosis of understanding, and this diagnosis is the least reliable part of practice [57]. That is a warning about the difficulty of the judgement, not about its value.

The assistance dilemma gives the trade-off a measurable form. Koedinger and Aleven's experiments with cognitive tutors varied how much help was given, when, and in what form; the consistent result is that assistance choices shift the balance between performance during practice and learning that survives it, so "more help" is not a coherent design goal [27]. The sharpest version of this is expertise reversal: support that compensates for missing schemas becomes redundant load once the schemas exist, and redundancy costs learning [21]. Soderstrom and Bjork's review explains why this is easy to miss in product metrics: performance during training is a biased index of learning, and conditions that slow the learner down often produce more durable outcomes [54].

Two lines of work push against the assumption that the AI should resolve difficulty. Kapur's productive failure studies show that attempting ill-structured problems before instruction, and failing, can prepare students to learn more from subsequent instruction than being taught the well-structured procedure first [22]. Aleven and colleagues' review of help-seeking shows the complementary behavioural fact: learners in interactive environments do not use help well — they avoid it when it would threaten self-esteem, request it when the answer is within reach, and rarely use it strategically [2]. Roll and colleagues showed that this is trainable: metacognitive feedback aimed at help-seeking behaviour improved both the behaviour and subsequent learning [47].

Where does that leave "the learner retains control"? It leaves it as a value, not a mechanism. Learner control is well motivated — autonomy support, avoidance of dependency, respect for the learner's own goals — but it is not self-executing, because the population least likely to request help appropriately is precisely the population that most needs it [2]. Any design in which the learner must initiate inherits that bias.

What this area establishes

  • Assistance effects are contingent on the learner's current knowledge and on the moment of intervention, and can reverse sign [21, 27].
  • Performance and learning must be measured separately, and they frequently diverge [54].
  • Learners' spontaneous help-seeking is systematically suboptimal, and trainable [2].

What it does not establish

  • That an AI should decide when to help. Almost all of this work studies human tutors or tutors with hand-authored, narrow-domain models; the judgement of "current understanding" was made by a person or by a domain model, not by a general-purpose language model.
  • That withholding help is better than offering it. The productive-failure results concern the sequence of generation and instruction, not the behaviour of a partner who could intervene at any moment.

4.2 Co-regulation and human–AI collaboration

The regulatory framing is the most useful available vocabulary for what Lemma wants, because it locates the unit of analysis between two parties rather than inside either. Järvelä and Hadwin's tripartite distinction — self-, co-, and socially shared regulation — separates three things that product language tends to blur: a learner regulating their own work; a partner temporarily supporting that regulation; and a group whose regulation is jointly owned [20]. Lemma's AI is designed for the middle category, but its workspace ambitions pull it toward the third, and the third requires capacities (shared goal commitment, socio-emotional regulation, negotiated task ownership) that an AI does not have.

Co-regulation has an identifiable microstructure. Volet's analyses of collaborative learning found that high-level co-regulation involves one partner addressing the other's planning, monitoring or evaluation — not merely answering the content question — and that it emerged in some groups and then decayed [59]. Roschelle and Teasley's joint problem space explains why decay is the default: the shared representation has to be continuously maintained, and it degrades when participants stop checking it [48].

Grounding theory supplies the cost model for that maintenance. Clark and Brennan's formulation — that grounding requires the participants to reach mutual belief at a cost paid jointly, and that the medium determines which costs dominate [11] — is arguably the single most useful framework for the workspace claim, because it predicts exactly which costs a shared visual context removes. When both parties can see the object, reference can be short and deixis replaces description; when they cannot, the same reference requires a description, a check, and often a repair [10].

Molenaar's hybrid human-AI regulation is the clearest existing attempt to move this into AI design, using a detect–diagnose–act structure in which the target of support is the learner's regulatory process rather than the task content [34]. Its importance for Lemma is that it reframes the participation question: the AI is not choosing whether to help with the mathematics, it is choosing whether to support planning, monitoring or evaluation, and those have different interruption costs.

Sebanz, Bekkering and Knoblich's work on joint action adds a constraint from motor cognition: co-representation of a partner's task is automatic and can interfere with one's own performance [51]. In a learning setting this predicts a cost that is usually invisible — knowing that a partner is watching and may act changes how the learner represents the task, even when the partner does nothing. This is the theoretical basis for treating "AI stays quiet but present" as a distinct condition from "no AI", not as a control condition.

4.3 Mixed-initiative interaction and selective participation

Mixed initiative is the area with the most directly usable structure, and also the one with the largest gap between theory and learning evidence. Horvitz's 1999 paper remains the canonical statement: the system should act when the expected value of acting exceeds the expected cost, where value is discounted by the probability that the system's inference about the user's needs is correct, and cost includes the user's effort to notice, interpret and correct the action [18]. Three of his principles are effectively design requirements for Lemma: consider uncertainty about the user's goals explicitly; use cheap interaction to reduce that uncertainty rather than guessing; and make correction cheap because some guesses will be wrong.

The lineage matters. Carbonell's SCHOLAR system already embedded the idea that a tutoring program should take initiative when the student's state warranted it and yield when it did not [8]; Allen, Guinn and Horvitz generalised the concept across dialogue systems [3]; Terveen framed human–computer collaboration as a matter of shared goals and adjustable initiative rather than command [56]. The idea is therefore not new to AI, and its repeated re-discovery in conversational products has not been matched by evidence about learning.

Automation research supplies the control vocabulary that mixed initiative lacks. Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens separate what is automated from how much, and make the crucial point that automation does not replace human activity so much as restructure it, creating new coordination demands [42]. Parasuraman and Riley's taxonomy of use, misuse, disuse and abuse names the ways operators adapt badly: over-trust, complacency, abandonment [41]. Endsley and Kiris show the structural consequence of high automation: with the operator out of the loop, situation awareness degrades and takeover is slow and error-prone [14].

For a learning agent, the "out-of-the-loop" finding has an exact analogue that the literature has not tested: a learner who has ceded the monitoring function to the AI may be unable to resume it when the AI is unavailable, wrong, or simply silent. This is not the same claim as "dependency"; it is a claim about situation awareness of one's own problem state, and it is testable with existing instruments.

Klein and colleagues' "team player" framing is the best available corrective to purely probabilistic accounts. Their ten challenges include a basic compact to participate in joint activity, directability (the human can redirect the agent), observability (the agent's state and intent are visible), and predictability [26]. The lesson for Lemma is that a well-calibrated intervenor that cannot be redirected is not a team player. Amershi's guidelines translate these into product-level obligations, notably: make clear what the system can do; make clear why it did what it did; support efficient invocation and dismissal; and adapt cautiously over time [5].

Shneiderman and Maes' debate is worth keeping in view because it anticipates the current argument. Maes argued for agents that act on the user's behalf; Shneiderman argued that direct manipulation preserves control and predictability and that autonomous agents shift the burden of monitoring onto the user [52]. Lemma's workspace is the direct-manipulation side of that debate and its voice layer is the agent side; the paper's value is that it predicts the specific friction at the seam.

The gap that matters most. Mixed-initiative theory tells you how to decide, but the value term it assumes is the user's task utility. Learning value is not task utility: the contribution that maximises the quality of the current solution may minimise the learning. Every application of Horvitz's framework to education therefore requires replacing the value term, and no paper in this corpus does so empirically.

4.4 Attention, cognitive load and interruption

Interruption research is where the cost side of Lemma's ledger is quantified. Its central finding is that the cost of an interruption is not a property of its content but of its relation to the task's structure. Adamczyk and Bailey varied only the moment of interruption within an identical task and found differences in performance, annoyance, and the social attribution the user made about the interrupter; boundaries between subtasks were consistently safer than moments inside one [1]. Monk, Trafton and Boehm-Davis showed that resumption time rises with both the duration and the cognitive demand of the interruption, and that this profile is consistent with a memory-for-goals account in which the suspended goal decays and must be reinstated [4, 35]. Iqbal and Horvitz's field data show the same structure outside the laboratory: interruptions in real work produce measurable resumption lags and frequently derail the suspended task altogether [19].

Two additional findings make the accounting worse than a simple delay. Leroy's attention-residue experiments show that switching costs persist after the person has returned, because part of attention remains with the interrupting task [30]. And Bailey and Konstan's measurements show that even brief interruptions raise error rates and negative affect, not just completion time [6]. A conversational intervention, which requires the learner to parse a sentence, decide whether it is relevant, and formulate a response, is not brief in this sense.

The countervailing evidence is that interruption cost is not uniform across channels. Multiple-resource theory holds that tasks drawing on different perceptual and cognitive resources interfere less than tasks drawing on the same ones [61]. Speech input and output occupy auditory and verbal resources; visual workspace manipulation occupies visual and spatial resources. On this account, a short spoken intervention during visual manipulation should cost less than a text panel that must be read in the same visual field. This is the strongest theoretical argument for voice in Lemma — and it is a theory about resource competition, not about naturalness.

The same theory predicts where voice fails. If the learner is reading prose, the verbal channel is already occupied, and spoken assistance competes directly with the material. The modality effect — better learning from spoken rather than written text accompanying diagrams [15] — holds for short, self-paced segments and reverses when the audio is long and transient, because the learner cannot re-inspect what has already been said [28]. Singh, Marcus and Ayres showed that segmenting transient audio mitigates but does not eliminate the disadvantage [53]. The practical implication is unusually clear: speech is a bad container for content the learner may need to revisit.

Two further lines bear on the "continuous help" worry. Risko and Gilbert's review of cognitive offloading shows that people offload strategically to reduce effort, and that offloading is often beneficial for the immediate task while reducing the internal representations needed later [46]. Lee and colleagues' survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking and a shift from producing to verifying, with participants describing the effort as moving rather than disappearing [29]. Neither study is about learning, and neither measures retention; both are consistent with the concern that continuous availability changes what the user does with their own mind.

4.5 Voice as an interaction modality

The literature on voice is less about speech than about modality assignment: which content belongs in which channel. Oviatt's work is the most useful corrective to assumptions that speech is natural and therefore preferable. Her "ten myths" paper argues, among other things, that users do not uniformly prefer speech, that multimodal integration is technically and cognitively complex, and that the modality that looks easiest for the designer is often the one that imposes most work on the user [40].

The empirical basis for assignment comes from studies of spontaneous multimodal interaction. Users given both speech and pen do not use them interchangeably: they reserve speech for descriptive and semantic content and pen for spatial and directly manipulable content, and they switch when the content type changes [13, 39]. This is a finding about fit, and it maps unusually well onto Lemma's architecture. Naming an assumption, stating an intention, accepting or rejecting a suggestion: descriptive, speech-suited. Selecting a term, adjusting a parameter, pointing at a step: spatial, manipulation-suited.

The counter-evidence is about expectation. Luger and Sellen's interviews with conversational-agent users documented a persistent gap between the promise of conversation and the experience of command-and-control: participants described the agents as brittle, limited, and socially awkward — "like having a really bad PA" [32]. Porcheron and colleagues' month-long in-home study showed that voice use is embedded in social accountability: people design their utterances for overhearers, repair the device's understanding in front of others, and treat speech as a public act rather than a private command [44]. Clark and colleagues' analysis of conversational quality reaches a related conclusion: conversational interaction is not a universal ideal, and users often prefer efficient, non-conversational interaction for instrumental tasks [12].

Nass and Moon's finding that people apply social rules to computers — politeness, reciprocity, gender stereotyping — matters here in a way that is easy to overlook [37]. If a spoken agent is treated as a social actor, then rejecting its suggestion is not a neutral interface action; it is a mildly social one. That cuts both ways for Lemma: social presence may increase engagement, and it may also raise the cost of saying "no", which is precisely the control action H4 depends on.

What does not exist. No study in this corpus examines voice as a coordination channel over a shared visual learning workspace. The nearest analogues are multimodal desktop systems from the 1990s and medical or field settings; the nearest learning evidence is about spoken versus written instructional text, which is output, not coordination. H6 is therefore a design hypothesis with plausible mechanisms and no direct test — and the report classifies it that way.

4.6 Workspace grounding and shared context

Referring is a collaborative process, not a labelling act. Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs showed that speakers and addressees iterate on a description until they reach a version both accept, and that they do so under a principle of least collaborative effort: the pair minimises total effort, not the speaker's effort [10]. In their tangram task, references became shorter and more conventional across trials as the pair accumulated shared history. Two consequences follow for Lemma. First, a learner saying "this assumption" is behaving optimally, not vaguely. Second, the system's ability to resolve such references is not a convenience feature; it is the precondition for the interaction being cheap enough to be worth having.

Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks' analysis of repair adds a normative constraint that design tends to violate. In conversation there is a strong preference for self-repair: participants give the speaker of a trouble-source the first opportunity to fix it, and other-repair is dispreferred [49]. Translated to a learning workspace, an AI that immediately corrects a learner's misstatement is doing other-repair at the earliest possible point — the interactional equivalent of interrupting. The preference structure suggests a cheaper and less face-threatening move: mark the trouble and let the learner repair it.

The workspace itself is not passive. Zhang and Norman showed that external representations change the difficulty and even the form of a cognitive task, so that what a person can do depends on how the problem is laid out [63]. Kirsh and Maglio showed that some actions in a workspace are performed to change the cognitive situation rather than the physical one — epistemic rather than pragmatic action [25]. Together these imply that a shared workspace is a cognitive resource jointly operated, not a display surface for a dialogue. An AI that treats the workspace as context for its utterances misses the point; the workspace is where a large part of the thinking happens.

Whittaker's review of "talking about things" in mediated communication provides the most directly relevant empirical summary: speech alone is often sufficient for effective conversation; visual information about the objects of work is generally more valuable than visual information about the participants; and disjoint visual perspectives undermine communication [60]. For a system that has both speech and a shared view of the objects, this is close to a design brief.

Suchman's critique of plan-based models of action is the theoretical reason not to over-intellectualise the learner's intent [55]. Plans are resources for situated action, not determinants of it; what a person is doing is observable in the specifics of the situation rather than derivable from a plan the system has inferred. An AI that grounds its interpretation in workspace state and recent action is following Suchman's advice; one that grounds it in a model of the learner's "learning objective" is not.

4.7 Long-term collaboration and continuity

This is the thinnest area for direct evidence and the one where Lemma's ambitions most exceed the literature. Olson and Olson's decade of field and laboratory work concluded that the decisive variable in geographically distributed collaboration is not bandwidth but common ground — and that distance matters because it raises the cost of maintaining it [38]. Their framework lists the factors that make collaboration "ready": common ground, coupling of work, collaboration technology readiness, and culture. Applied to a long-horizon AI partner, the framework predicts that continuity is valuable exactly to the extent that it reduces the cost of re-establishing common ground — and that accumulated, unverified context can be worse than none, because it produces false confidence in shared understanding.

Transactive memory supplies the mechanism. Lewis's validated scale decomposes a transactive memory system into specialisation (who knows what), credibility (how much to trust each member's expertise) and coordination (how well access is orchestrated) [31]. Human teams with well-developed transactive systems perform better. A learner working with an AI may develop a transactive relation in which the AI is assumed to hold certain knowledge — and the credibility component is the risk, because it must be calibrated by experience, and language models are confidently wrong at a rate that makes experience a noisy teacher.

Collaborative memory research adds a caution against assuming that shared records improve memory. Rajaram and Pereira-Pasarin's review documents both benefits (error correction, transactive gain) and costs (retrieval disruption, collaborative inhibition — groups recall less than the sum of their members' individual recall) [45]. The design implication is that a persistent record of prior reasoning is not automatically an aid: it can anchor the learner to a faulty earlier framing, and it can substitute for the reconstructive effort that produces durable memory.

Schmidt and Bannon's concept of articulation work names the invisible labour of coordinating a distributed activity — scheduling, aligning representations, repairing breakdowns [50]. A collaboration partner is valuable to the extent that it absorbs articulation work rather than generating it. This gives a clean test for any continuity feature: does it reduce the work the learner must do to re-enter their own prior thinking, or does it add a surface the learner must now maintain?

Honest gap. Four of the five works in this area study human teams. The one AI-specific source [34] is conceptual. There is no longitudinal study in this corpus that measures learning outcomes of a persistent AI partner across sessions, and the product claim in H5 should be treated as unvalidated rather than as supported by analogy.

Key paper 1 · Area I

The role of tutoring in problem solving

Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976). Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 17(2), 89–100. [62]

Problem
What does a tutor actually do that a learner cannot do alone, and how is that support organised in interaction?
Theory
Scaffolding as a contingent, interactional achievement rather than a property of a task or a tool; the learner is treated as constructing competence inside a supported region.
Method
Observational analysis of tutorial interaction with young children on a construction task; qualitative identification of tutorial functions.
Findings
Six functions characterise effective tutoring: recruiting interest, reducing degrees of freedom, maintaining direction, marking critical features, controlling frustration, and demonstrating. Each is contingent — its value depends on the learner's state at that moment, and the same act can be helpful or intrusive.
Limits
Small-sample, single-task observation with a human tutor; no outcome measures, no comparison condition, no account of how the tutor's diagnosis is made.
Relevance
Supplies the functional vocabulary for what an AI's contributions should be for — not "answering" but recruiting, narrowing, marking, and demonstrating. Its central claim is also the strongest argument against fixed intervention policies: contingency cannot be pre-scheduled.

Theoretical   Basis: abstract; the six functions are the paper's canonical contribution.

Key paper 2 · Area I

Exploring the assistance dilemma in experiments with cognitive tutors

Koedinger & Aleven (2007). Educational Psychology Review, 19(3), 239–264. [27]

Problem
When should an instructional system provide assistance, and how much, given that assistance can both enable and displace learning?
Theory
Cognitive load theory and the "assistance dilemma": help trades immediate success against the cognitive processing that produces learning.
Method
Programmatic review of experiments run within the Cognitive Tutor / Andes lineage, plus a methodological argument about how to design studies that separate performance from learning.
Findings
Assistance choices reliably change the performance–learning balance; there is no global optimum. The paper argues that the field's default justification — "help helps" — conflates what learners accomplish with what they learn, and proposes experimental designs that hold task success constant while varying the cognitive work required.
Limits
Confined to well-structured quantitative domains with step-level models; the "learning" measures are mostly near-transfer post-tests; no treatment of open-ended or creative work.
Relevance
This is the paper that makes Lemma's H1 coherent: the argument for selective participation is not that help is bad but that help has a hidden cost that only delayed measurement reveals. It also explains why a product optimising session success will drift toward over-assistance.

Indirect   Basis: abstract and the framework's subsequent use in the tutoring literature.

Key paper 3 · Area I

The expertise reversal effect

Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003). Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 23–31. [21]

Problem
Why do instructional techniques that work for novices fail, or reverse, for more knowledgeable learners?
Theory
Cognitive load theory: once schemas exist, instructional support that compensates for their absence becomes redundant and consumes working memory that could be used for learning.
Method
Review and synthesis of experiments across domains (including technical and physics instruction) comparing supported and unsupported formats at different levels of prior knowledge.
Findings
Support that is highly effective for low-knowledge learners loses effectiveness and can produce worse outcomes for high-knowledge learners. The reversal is not a small interaction; in several studies the sign of the effect changes.
Limits
Expertise is usually operationalised by a domain test administered at one point; the effect is documented mostly for structured instructional materials, not for dialogue or assistance in open tasks.
Relevance
Directly constrains any "adaptive" participation policy: adaptation must be to what the learner has automated, not to task difficulty or to error rate. It also predicts that a system tuned on novice behaviour will become intrusive as the same learner gains competence — the fading problem, in a sharper form.

Key paper 4 · Area I

Productive failure

Kapur (2008). Cognition and Instruction, 26(3), 379–424. [22]

Problem
Can attempting problems before receiving instruction — and failing — produce better learning than being taught the procedure first?
Theory
Generation and failure as preparation for learning: prior attempts build the representational resources that later instruction can organise.
Method
Randomised classroom study with eleventh-grade science students in a computer-supported collaborative setting; two sequences compared (ill-structured then well-structured vs. well-structured throughout), with individual post-tests.
Findings
The generation-first groups produced poor solutions and struggled to define and analyse the problems, yet outperformed the comparison group on subsequent well-structured problem solving. The failure was real and the benefit was delayed, appearing after instruction rather than during the generative phase.
Limits
One topic (Newtonian kinematics), one age group, group-based generation; the mechanism is inferred rather than measured, and later work has shown the effect is sensitive to design details.
Relevance
Gives the strongest theoretical case for STAY QUIET during the learner's generative attempts. It also reframes what an AI should do after failure: not rescue the attempt, but use the attempt as the material for instruction. A tutor that prevents the failure removes the preparation.

Key paper 5 · Area I

Learning versus performance: An integrative review

Soderstrom & Bjork (2015). Perspectives on Psychological Science, 10(2), 176–199. [54]

Problem
Why is performance during instruction an unreliable index of whether learning has occurred?
Theory
The learning–performance distinction, grounded in desirable difficulties: conditions that slow acquisition often improve retention and transfer, and vice versa.
Method
Integrative review across experimental psychology, motor learning and education.
Findings
Numerous manipulations improve performance while impairing retention, and impair performance while improving retention. The review argues that instruction should be evaluated on durable changes in comprehension and skill, not on in-session success, and that learners' own judgements of learning are similarly biased.
Limits
Most primary evidence comes from laboratory tasks with short retention intervals; the translation to months-long learning in open domains is argued rather than demonstrated.
Relevance
This is the measurement rule for the whole Voice System. Any claim that a participation policy "works" must specify which of the four outcomes it improves — in-session performance, learning during the task, independent performance afterwards, or transfer — because these can move in opposite directions.

Key paper 6 · Area I

Help seeking and help design in interactive learning environments

Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer & Wallace (2003). Review of Educational Research, 73(3), 277–320. [2]

Problem
Do learners use on-demand help effectively, and what determines whether they do?
Theory
Help-seeking as a metacognitive and motivational strategy, situated between self-regulated learning and the design of the help system.
Method
Selective review of studies in interactive learning environments, organised by system factors, learner factors, and their interaction.
Findings
Effective help-seeking is associated with better learning, but learners frequently do not seek help when they need it, and seek it in ways that do not support learning. The review identifies help avoidance (driven by self-esteem and social comparison), executive help-seeking (asking for the answer), and instrumental help-seeking (asking for the principle) as distinct patterns, and argues that design and metacognitive skill interact.

Review rather than meta-analysis; the primary studies largely concern structured domains and menu-based hint systems, not open dialogue.
Relevance
It is the empirical basis for Lemma's central tension. H4 assumes learners can steer participation; this paper shows the steering behaviour is systematically biased. A purely learner-initiated design will under-serve the learners who most need support, and a proactive design must avoid training executive help-seeking.

Key paper 7 · Area I

The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems

VanLehn (2011). Educational Psychologist, 46(4), 197–221. [58]

Problem
How much does tutoring help, and does finer-grained interaction produce larger gains?
Theory
The "interaction hypothesis" — that effectiveness depends on the granularity of the interaction loop — tested against the alternative that the tutor's knowledge or pedagogy is what matters.
Method
Review of controlled experiments comparing human tutoring, computer tutoring and no tutoring, with computer systems classified by interaction granularity (answer-based, step-based, substep-based).
Findings
Tutoring of any kind beats no tutoring, but the curve flattens: answer-based systems are the weakest, and step-based and substep-based systems are roughly comparable to each other and to human tutors. The strong version of the interaction hypothesis — that more granular dialogue yields proportionally more learning — is not supported.
Limits
Confined to well-structured domains with measurable steps; comparisons are across studies rather than within one design; "human tutoring" varies enormously in quality.
Relevance
Sets a realistic expectation for Lemma: the marginal value of a richer conversational layer is not guaranteed to exceed the value of a well-structured step-level interaction. It argues for measuring the incremental benefit of voice and initiative over a competent non-conversational baseline.

Key paper 8 · Area I

Active-constructive-interactive: A conceptual framework for differentiating learning activities

Chi (2009). Topics in Cognitive Science, 1(1), 73–105. [9]

Problem
Are "active", "constructive" and "interactive" distinct, and do they predict different learning outcomes?
Theory
A framework that defines the three in terms of observable overt activity and the underlying processes it licenses, generating a testable ordering hypothesis.
Method
Conceptual analysis with a review of existing evidence; the paper's contribution is definitional and predictive rather than a new experiment.
Findings
The three are distinguishable, and the framework predicts interactive > constructive > active for learning. Interaction is not merely more engaging; it requires the learner to produce inferences and have them taken up by a partner.

The ordering is a hypothesis, not an established law; "interactive" is defined in a way that requires a genuinely responsive partner, which is exactly what is uncertain about an AI.

Gives Lemma a principled reason to want interaction rather than polished explanation: the value is in what the learner must generate. It also implies a design test for each AI turn — does it elicit a constructive or interactive response, or does it merely elicit acknowledgement?

Key paper 9 · Area I

Kestin, Miller, Klales, Milbourne & Ponti (2025). Scientific Reports, 15, Article 17458. [23]

Problem
How does a purpose-built generative AI tutor compare with a well-taught active-learning class on learning and on learner experience?
Theory
Pedagogical design, not model capability, determines tutoring quality; the AI tutor was built to implement the same research-based practices used in the comparison class.

Students learned significantly more in significantly less time with the AI tutor than in the active-learning class, and reported higher engagement and motivation. The strongest pro-AI-tutoring evidence available, and simultaneously the strongest argument for H1's mechanism: what produced the gain was a tutor that refused to behave like a chatbot. Key paper 10 · Area I

Problem

Tool design as the operative variable: an unconstrained assistant supports task completion, while a constrained one is intended to protect the learner's own processing.

Conceptual synthesis of regulation research and CSCL design, with illustrative process data.

Key paper 12 · Area II

Key paper 14 · Area III

Principles of mixed-initiative user interfaces

Problem
When should an intelligent system act on its own, and when should it defer to the user?
Theory
Expected utility of initiative: value of the action, discounted by the probability that the system's inference about the user's goals is correct, minus the cost of the action including the user's cost of noticing, understanding and correcting it.
Method
Conceptual framework illustrated by the LookOut system for scheduling and meeting management; no controlled evaluation of the principles.
Findings
Twelve design principles, of which four carry most weight for Lemma: consider uncertainty about goals explicitly; weigh the costs and benefits of autonomous action; use cheap dialogue to reduce uncertainty rather than guessing; and make correction of wrong inferences inexpensive. The structural backbone of Section 6. Its limitation is Lemma's central research problem: the value term must be learning value, which is not observable in the session and is often negatively correlated with task utility.

Key paper 17 · Area IV

Key paper 18 · Area IV

Meta-analysis of the modality effect

Ginns (2005). Learning and Instruction, 15(4), 313–331. [15]

Problem
Is the modality effect — spoken rather than written text accompanying visual material — reliable, and what moderates it?
Theory
Cognitive load theory's modality principle: distributing processing across visual and auditory channels reduces load when the material is otherwise visually saturated.
Method
Meta-analysis of controlled experiments comparing audio-plus-visual with visual-only presentations.
Findings
A reliable overall advantage for the modality format, with substantial heterogeneity. The advantage is conditional on pacing and on the length of the spoken segment: longer, system-paced audio erodes it.
Limits
Primary studies use short instructional segments in laboratory or classroom settings; outcomes are immediate comprehension and transfer tests, not long-term retention; little coverage of interactive or contingent speech.
Relevance
The strongest quantitative support for voice as an output channel in Lemma — but only under conditions Lemma must engineer: short segments, learner control of pacing, and visual material that is genuinely visual.

Key paper 19 · Area IV

Cognitive load theory, modality of presentation and the transient information effect

Leahy & Sweller (2011). Applied Cognitive Psychology, 25(6), 943–951. [28]

Problem
When does the modality effect reverse, and why?
Theory
Transience: spoken information disappears and cannot be re-inspected, so long audio imposes working-memory costs that written text does not.
Method
Two experiments with primary-school students learning to read a temperature graph from either audio-plus-diagram or written-text-plus-diagram materials.
Findings
The visual-text condition outperformed the audio condition, reversing the usual modality effect. The authors attribute the reversal to the auditory text being too long and transient to be processed comfortably, so that its working-memory cost exceeded the benefit of freeing the visual channel.
Limits
Children, one task, short instructional materials; the manipulation confounds modality with transience, which is the point but also limits generalisation.
Relevance
The single most important constraint on Lemma's voice output: speech is a poor medium for content the learner may need to revisit, and the boundary is not "short vs long" in words but "can the learner re-inspect it". It predicts that spoken explanations of more than a sentence or two will underperform equivalent on-screen text.

Key paper 21 · Area VI

Clark & Brennan (1991). In Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition (pp. 127–149). APA. [11]

Problem
What does it take for two participants to establish that they have understood each other, and how do media change that work?
Theory
Grounding: communication succeeds when participants reach mutual belief that understanding has occurred, at a cost paid jointly; media differ in the costs they impose.
Method
Conceptual framework with analysis of media properties (copresence, visibility, audibility, cotemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, reviewability, revisability).
Findings
Grounding is collaborative and cost-sensitive; the least collaborative effort principle selects the cheapest sufficient evidence of understanding. Problem

Synthesis of a decade of field and laboratory studies of collocated and remote collaboration.

Section 6Integrated framework

The framework below is not a model of the learner or an algorithm. It is a statement of which variables the participation decision depends on, which theory supplies each variable, and how confident we can be that the variable matters.

6.2 The decision is five dimensions, not three states

ASK, OFFER and STAY QUIET are projections of a higher-dimensional decision onto one axis. 6.4 Two structural problems the framework exposes

The accounting asymmetry

False interventions and false silences are not symmetric in a product. An unnecessary interruption is immediate, attributable and often complained about; a missed opportunity for support is invisible, because the learner simply continues with a worse plan and no one observes the counterfactual. The measurement literature makes this worse: in-session performance rises with assistance [7, 27], so the metric that teams watch moves in the direction of more intervention. Nothing in the literature corrects for this, and it is the single most likely reason a well-intentioned selective system drifts toward an always-on assistant.

The legibility problem

Grounding requires evidence of understanding, and in a human pair that evidence is produced continuously by both parties [11]. An AI that stays quiet produces no evidence of anything. The learner therefore cannot distinguish three states that require different responses: the AI understood and chose not to intervene; the AI did not understand the reference; the AI is not attending. Klein's observability requirement is the general form of the fix [26], and Amershi's guidance on making system state clear is the product form [5]. The specific implication for Lemma is stronger than either: silence must be rendered as a state, not as an absence.

Nardi's argument that mediated communication is about connection rather than bandwidth is relevant to how that state should be rendered [36]. Connection — the sense that a partner is present, attending, and committed — is not carried by information volume. A minimal, continuously visible indication of attention may do more for the learner's willingness to rely on the system than any amount of spoken explanation.

6.5 What this framework is not

It is not an algorithm, and the literature does not support building one yet. Four components would be required, and each is currently the weakest link in its own area: a calibrated estimate of the learner's automated repertoire (expertise reversal tells us this matters, not how to measure it); a detector of learning-relevant boundaries in open-ended work (interruption research assumes the boundaries exist); a model of learning value per act type (the assistance dilemma says this is the crux and supplies no function); and a policy that can be evaluated on delayed outcomes (almost no study in this corpus does this). Writing a decision rule now would encode guesses as if they were findings.

Section 7Critical evaluation of product hypotheses

Each hypothesis is assessed on three questions: what supports it, what contradicts it, and what observation would change the verdict. The last question is the one that makes the assessment useful.

H1 — Selective participation

Claim. An AI that deliberately chooses when not to intervene may support learning better than an AI that responds to every opportunity.

Support. The components are among the best-established findings in the corpus. Assistance that replaces generative work raises performance and lowers learning [27, 54]; the same support reverses sign with expertise [21]; generation attempts before instruction improve later learning [22]; and in the LLM era, unconstrained access produced a 17% decline in unaided performance while a safeguarded tutor largely avoided it [7]. The contrast between the safeguarded and unconstrained conditions in that experiment is the closest thing in the literature to a test of "selection matters".

Against. The hypothesis compares selective participation to "responding to every opportunity", but the realistic alternative is "responding when asked" — and that baseline fails for a different reason: learners do not ask well [2]. A system that withholds intervention inherits that failure. VanLehn's plateau finding also warns that richer, better-timed interaction does not reliably produce larger gains than competent step-level interaction [58], so the burden of proof is on the added complexity.

Verdict: partial support. The mechanisms are supported; the comparative claim is not. Nothing in the corpus shows that an AI's decision to stay silent improves learning relative to a well-designed learner-initiated system.

What would settle it. A three-arm randomised trial — always-answer, selective, learner-initiated-only — with identical content and a delayed transfer test. The mediator to measure is the learner's own help-seeking behaviour, because the mechanism claim is that selection trains better regulation, not merely that it reduces interruption.

H2 — Workspace grounding

Claim. Grounding spoken interaction in the learner's current workspace may reduce context reconstruction and improve collaboration quality.

Support. Grounding is cost-sensitive, and shared visual access removes the largest cost — describing referents [11]. Reference converges through iterative repair under least collaborative effort, which is only cheap when both parties can see the object [10]. Visual information about the objects of work is more valuable than visual information about the participants, and disjoint perspectives undermine communication [60]. External representations change the task itself, so the workspace is not neutral context [63].

Against. All of this evidence is human–human, and it assumes symmetrical access to evidence of understanding. The AI's grounding is one-sided: it can ground its utterance in the workspace, but it cannot verify that the learner has understood, and a confidently wrong resolution is worse than an explicit question because it is invisible until it produces a bad contribution. Distributed cognition's unit of analysis also cuts the other way: if the workspace is part of the cognitive system, then an AI reading the workspace is reading the learner's thinking, which raises a consent question the literature does not address [17].

Verdict: partial support. The cost model transfers; the mechanism by which an AI achieves grounding does not, and its failure mode is silent.

What would settle it. A within-subject comparison of the same AI policy with and without workspace access, measuring reference-resolution accuracy, the rate at which the learner must restate context, and the frequency of confidently wrong resolutions. "Context reconstruction" needs an operational definition — time-to-first-productive-move after a session break is a candidate.

H3 — Mixed initiative

Claim. Allowing both learner and AI to initiate may support more adaptive collaboration than purely user-initiated chat.

Support. Mixed initiative has a formal decision structure [18], a lineage in tutoring systems that predates chat [8], and a control vocabulary that separates type from level [42]. Team-player requirements specify what an initiating agent owes its partner: directability, observability, predictability [26]. Product guidelines converge on the same commitments [5].

Against. Proactive behaviour is the most robustly costly thing an interface can do. Timing alone changes performance and social attribution [1]; resumption cost scales with the interruption's demand [35]; residue persists after return [30]. Automation research documents the downstream failures of initiative: complacency, over-trust, and loss of situation awareness when the human must take over [14, 41]. Users of conversational agents report that unprompted agent behaviour is one of the main sources of disappointment [32], and the direct-manipulation critique predicts exactly this — delegation moves monitoring cost onto the user [52].

Verdict: partial support. The architecture is well motivated and the risks are well documented; what is missing is any learning-outcome evidence. Every empirical study cited here measured task performance, affect, or attitudes — not retention or transfer.

What would settle it. The same three-arm design as H1, with an added process measure: does AI-initiated interaction increase or decrease the learner's own initiation over time? A mixed-initiative system that progressively silences the learner has failed on its own terms.

H4 — Learner agency

Claim. Giving learners meaningful control over accepting, rejecting and redirecting AI participation may help preserve their agency and independent reasoning.

Support. Control is a design obligation in the human–AI guidelines (efficient invocation and dismissal) [5] and in the automation ladder, where human veto is a level, not an afterthought [42]. Directability is a defining property of a team player [26]. Social-response findings suggest that explicit, low-cost rejection is important precisely because rejecting a spoken agent carries social weight [37].

Against. This is the hypothesis with genuinely conflicting evidence rather than merely thin evidence. Learners do not regulate help-seeking well: they avoid help that threatens self-esteem, request answers instead of principles, and misjudge when they need support [2]. Help-seeking skill is trainable, which implies it is a capacity, not a given [47]. Worse, control without understanding can be illusory: operators given control at high automation levels still lost situation awareness [14], and the direct-manipulation debate turns on whether control actually restores comprehension or only the feeling of it [52].

Verdict: conflicting evidence. The hypothesis is defensible as a value commitment, but as an empirical claim about learning it is unresolved and has a serious counterargument.

The distinction that resolves the conflict. "The learner can veto" and "the learner must initiate" are different designs, and the evidence supports the first while undermining the second. A system with proactive but fully rejectable participation preserves agency without inheriting the under-helping bias. This distinction should be written into the product definition, because the current phrasing of H4 admits both readings.

What would settle it. A 2×2 design crossing who initiates with who can veto, measuring learning, voluntary help-seeking, and a validated agency or ownership measure — plus a manipulation check that learners can actually predict what the AI will do.

H5 — Long-term continuity

Claim. Maintaining relevant context across sessions may improve sustained learning and collaborative problem solving.

Support. Common ground is the limiting resource in sustained collaboration, and distance matters because maintaining it is expensive [38]. Transactive memory systems — specialisation, credibility, coordination — predict team performance [31]. Persistent records support talk about objects over time, and speech alone is often sufficient when the record is shared [60]. Articulation work is real labour that a partner can absorb [50].

Against. Collaborative memory is not simply additive: groups exhibit retrieval disruption and collaborative inhibition, and a shared record can anchor participants to an earlier, wrong framing [45]. Accumulated context also produces false confidence in shared understanding, which Olson's framework treats as a failure mode rather than a benefit [38]. And the mechanism Lemma's hypothesis implies — memory enables personalisation enables learning — is not the mechanism the evidence supports. The supported mechanism is narrower: memory reduces the cost of re-establishing common ground.

Verdict: insufficient evidence. Theoretically coherent, empirically unestablished for AI partners, and with a credible harm pathway that the product currently does not measure.

What would settle it. A crossover study over several weeks in which the AI's carried-forward context is (a) full, (b) absent, (c) present but learner-reviewable and correctable. Outcomes: time to re-ground at session start, delayed retention, and the rate at which the learner corrects the AI's representation of prior work.

H6 — Voice as coordination

Claim. Voice may be especially valuable as a low-friction coordination channel during ongoing cognitive activity, rather than as a replacement for text.

Support. The modality effect shows a reliable advantage for spoken text accompanying visual material [15]; multiple-resource theory explains why speech costs little when visual and manual resources are occupied [61]; and users spontaneously assign descriptive content to speech and spatial content to pen when both are available [13, 39]. Connection, not bandwidth, is what a communication channel supplies [36].

Against. The modality advantage reverses when spoken content is long and transient [28, 53] — and coordination utterances are short, which helps, but the same literature says the boundary is re-inspectability, not length. Users' experience of conversational agents is dominated by disappointment relative to the conversational promise [32], and speech in shared physical space is a public, accountable act [44]. Oviatt's myths paper warns specifically against assuming that speech is the natural or preferred modality [40], and Clark's analysis shows that conversational form is often not what users want for instrumental tasks [12].

Verdict: insufficient evidence. Not contradicted, but untested. Every supporting finding concerns either spoken instructional output or input efficiency in a multimodal desktop task. None concerns voice as a coordination layer over a shared learning workspace, which is the actual claim.

What would settle it. A within-subject experiment holding the AI policy constant and varying only channel: voice, inline text, and voice-with-deixis. Measure coordination latency (time from intention to successful redirection), repair rate, secondary-task performance as a load proxy, and delayed learning. The prediction from the theory is specific: voice should win on coordination latency and lose on content-heavy acts — if it does not, the coordination thesis is wrong for a reason worth knowing.

Section 8Unresolved questions

These are questions the literature does not answer and that Lemma cannot avoid answering in design. They are stated so that they can be investigated rather than assumed.

  1. Does silence have a learning benefit, or only a comfort benefit? No study isolates "the AI decided not to intervene" from "the AI was absent". The productive-failure evidence concerns sequence, not presence, so the benefit of a watching-but-quiet partner is inferred rather than shown.
  2. Can a learning-relevant boundary be detected in open-ended work? All breakpoint research assumes a task grammar. Mathematical exploration and research have soft boundaries; whether pause-and-edit patterns are a valid proxy is unknown.
  3. Is a proactive question better than a proactive explanation at equal information? Chi's framework predicts yes [9]; the assistance dilemma predicts the answer depends on expertise [27]. Neither has been tested with a language-model partner.
  4. Does voice interfere with the learner's own verbal processing? The mechanism is plausible from resource theory [61], and articulatory suppression is well documented, but no study measures the effect of an AI's speech on a learner's inner speech during reading or reasoning.
  5. What is the right granularity of participation control? Per act, per session, per task type, per learner? Guidelines recommend dismissal [5]; nothing establishes whether learners want to configure participation or merely to veto it.
  6. Does persistent context improve re-grounding or entrench a framing? Both are predicted by different parts of the same literature [38, 45], and the sign may depend on whether the learner can inspect and correct what is carried forward.
  7. How should the AI display uncertainty without adding noise? Explanation requirements [5] and observability [26] both imply visibility; neither says how often a confidence signal can be shown before it becomes another interruption.
  8. Is "co-regulation" with a non-regulating agent a coherent construct? Regulation theory assumes a partner with its own goals and self-regulation [20]. If the AI has neither, what Lemma calls co-regulation may be better described as contingent tool use — which would change what should be measured.

10.1 What follows for the three modes

AI ASKS. Questions are the highest-value, highest-cost act: they preserve generation [9] and they demand a reply, which extends the interruption [35]. They should therefore be rare, short, and placed at boundaries. The design question is not "what should the AI ask?" but "what is the smallest question that changes what the learner does next?"

AI OFFERS. Offers are cheaper because they do not require uptake, but they are also easier to ignore and easier to over-produce. Their natural form is not a sentence in a chat log but a mark on the object — closer to an annotation than to a message, which also makes the offer re-inspectable and removes the transience penalty [28].

AI STAYS QUIET. This is the mode that most needs engineering. As specified, it is indistinguishable from a system that is confused or absent. Rendering it as an observable state is the minimum condition for it to function as a collaboration signal, and the cost of getting this wrong is that the learner attributes the system's silence to their own incomprehensibility — a failure mode with no instrumentation.

10.2 What the evidence does not let Lemma claim

  • That voice improves learning. No study supports this in a shared workspace, and the transience evidence predicts specific harms for long spoken content.
  • That silence improves learning. The mechanism is plausible and the comparative test has not been run.
  • That proactive intervention is better than learner-initiated help. The strongest direct evidence supports constrained assistance, not AI-initiated assistance [7, 23].
  • That long-term memory of a learner improves outcomes. The mechanism the evidence supports is cheaper re-grounding, which is a different and smaller claim.
  • That conversation is the wrong container for collaboration. This is a hypothesis about interaction design, and the evidence is consistent with it without establishing it.

10.3 Instrumentation this implies

Three measurements follow directly from the review and are not currently standard in AI tutoring products. First, a silence ledger: every decision not to act, with its context, sampled and coded for missed opportunity — without it, the accounting asymmetry in Section 6.4 guarantees drift. Second, a re-grounding measure: time from session start to the learner's first productive move, which is the operational form of the H2 and H5 claims. Third, paired outcome measurement: every in-session performance metric accompanied by a delayed or transfer measure, because the two are known to diverge [54].
